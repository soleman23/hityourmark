The Region

The Vessel and the
Naturalist Are Everything

Polar expedition travel is the category where the planning decisions have the largest consequences. The vessel determines which landings are accessible — a ship with ice-strengthened hull and shallow draft reaches coastlines that larger vessels cannot approach. The naturalist team determines what you understand about what you're seeing — an ornithologist who has been studying the same penguin colony for twenty years, a glaciologist who can describe what the calving timeline of a specific glacier wall tells us about the pace of polar change, a Zodiac pilot who positions the inflatable in the right place at the right moment when the whale surfaces.

Antarctica between November and March is the primary window — penguin nesting and hatching, whale feeding in the Drake Passage, and the long polar days that give explorers eighteen-hour light windows for landings. The Antarctic Peninsula is the most accessible route; South Georgia and the Falklands extend the itinerary into one of the most extraordinary wildlife concentrations anywhere — king penguins in colonies of a quarter million, elephant seals, albatross nesting grounds.

The Arctic in summer brings polar bears on the ice, walrus haul-outs on remote beaches, and the midnight sun that turns the Norwegian fjords gold at two in the morning. Winter in the Arctic means the northern lights — a phenomenon that rewards patience, cold tolerance, and a location far enough north that the aurora appears directly overhead rather than on the horizon.

Destination Photography · Antarctica
Southern Ocean
Antarctica
The last true wilderness on earth. A continent with no permanent human population, no roads, no artificial light, and wildlife — penguins, leopard seals, humpback whales, albatross — that has evolved with no experience of predators and therefore has no fear of the humans who arrive by Zodiac and stand at a respectful distance while it goes about its day. The Antarctic Peninsula — accessible from Ushuaia at the tip of Argentina — is the most traveled route; South Georgia adds king penguin colonies of 250,000 animals and Ernest Shackleton's grave at Grytviken. A small-ship expedition vessel with a maximum of 100 passengers and a naturalist-to-guest ratio that allows for genuine interpretation of everything encountered. Best November through March.

Arctic Norway & Northern Lights
The Norwegian Arctic coastline above the polar circle — Tromsø as the base for northern lights viewing between September and March, the Lofoten Islands for a landscape that defies what islands are supposed to look like, and the Hurtigruten coastal route for a working ship that has been delivering mail to Norwegian fishing villages since 1893. The northern lights require darkness, which means the season runs from late September through late March when the sun stays below the horizon. A combination of a dark-sky location, a guide who understands aurora activity cycles, and patience produces one of the most extraordinary natural phenomena visible from earth.

Destination Photography · Svalbard
High Arctic
Svalbard
The Norwegian archipelago halfway between mainland Norway and the North Pole — the only place in the world where polar bears outnumber human residents. A small-ship expedition from Longyearbyen covers the fjords, the tidewater glaciers, and the walrus haul-outs on the remote north coast that are accessible only to vessels small enough to navigate pack ice. The midnight sun in June and July means twenty-four hours of light and an extraordinary quality of photography at two in the morning when the low sun angles across the glacier faces. A combination of Svalbard and Arctic Norway in the same trip produces the full range of high Arctic experience in under two weeks.

Destination Photography · Greenland
North Atlantic Arctic
Greenland
The world's largest island — 80% covered by an ice sheet that contains enough fresh water to raise global sea levels by seven meters if it melted entirely. The Ilulissat Icefjord, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, produces some of the largest icebergs in the Northern Hemisphere as the Sermeq Kujalleq glacier — one of the fastest-moving glaciers in the world — calves into Disko Bay at a rate visible to a patient observer. Dog sledding in the east coast settlements in April, before the sea ice breaks up. An expedition vessel along the west coast in July and August for iceberg photography at scale. Best June through September for general travel; April through May for dog sledding and ice culture.

Destination Photography · Iceland
North Atlantic
Iceland
The most accessible polar-adjacent destination in the world — three hours from London, five from New York, and a landscape that compresses glaciers, volcanoes, geothermal fields, waterfalls, and the northern lights into an area smaller than Kentucky. The Golden Circle and the south coast are the obvious itinerary; the Westfjords are the Iceland that most visitors never find — a peninsula of fjords and cliffs that feels genuinely remote and produces arctic fox encounters, puffin colonies, and a silence that the Ring Road circuit doesn't provide. Best in summer for the midnight sun and the full landscape; best in winter for the northern lights and the ice caves in Vatnajökull glacier. Year-round is genuinely excellent for different reasons.

South Atlantic
Falklands & South Georgia
The two most extraordinary wildlife destinations in the South Atlantic — and the natural extension of an Antarctica expedition for travelers who want to go further. South Georgia for the king penguin colonies of a quarter million animals, the elephant seal beaches, and Shackleton's grave at Grytviken — the polar explorer buried on the island he struggled to reach on one of the most remarkable small-boat journeys in human history. The Falkland Islands for the gentoo and rockhopper penguins nesting within walking distance of Stanley's capital, and a landscape that combines British colonial infrastructure with the wildest sub-Antarctic terrain accessible by scheduled flight. Best November through February in the Austral summer.

Why Plan With Us

What We Add to Polar Expeditions

Polar expedition travel has no equivalent planning stakes anywhere in travel. The vessel, the naturalist team, and the specific berth category are everything. Getting these right is the work.

⬡
Vessel Selection
Not all Antarctic expedition vessels are equal — and the difference between a 100-passenger ship with an ice-strengthened hull and a 500-passenger vessel with shore excursions is the difference between an expedition and a cruise. We know the operators who carry the right naturalist teams, the vessels with the shallow drafts that reach the coastlines no other ship can approach, and the operators whose expedition ethos means the naturalist drives the itinerary rather than the entertainment schedule.
⬡
Naturalist Team Quality
The naturalist is to polar travel what the guide is to African safari — the single most consequential variable in the quality of the experience. An ornithologist who has been studying the same penguin colony for twenty years. A glaciologist who can explain what the calving timeline tells us about polar change. A historian of exploration whose Shackleton narratives make the Drake Passage crossing into its own experience. These are the naturalists on the vessels we recommend.
⬡
Berth & Cabin Selection
Polar expedition vessels have a range of cabin categories — from interior lower-deck berths to expedition suites with forward-facing windows. The cabin category matters in Antarctica in ways it doesn't in other sea travel: a forward-facing window on an ice transit means a view that most passengers don't have. A deck-level cabin means a walk to the Zodiac embarkation platform rather than three flights of stairs in a survival suit. We advise on cabin selection as an integrated part of expedition planning.
⬡
Pre- & Post-Expedition Planning
An Antarctica expedition requires arriving in Ushuaia at least a day before embarkation — weather delays happen, and missing embarkation is a non-refundable consequence of inadequate buffer. We build the pre-expedition logistics — flights, accommodation, transfers — with the same rigor as the expedition itself. Post-expedition, most clients want two or three nights in Buenos Aires to decompress before the return flight. We plan the full trip, not just the vessel berth.
When to Go

Polar Regions by Season

Antarctica Window
November – March
The only viable Antarctic expedition window. November brings the earliest penguin nesting activity and the fewest vessels on the peninsula — the quietest, most pristine version of the continent. December and January are peak season with the highest wildlife activity: penguin chick hatching, whale feeding, and the longest days. February and March offer calving glacier activity and the first penguin juveniles beginning to swim. The Drake Passage is notoriously unpredictable in all months — be prepared for both crossings regardless of departure date.
Antarctica Window
Arctic Summer
June – August
The primary Arctic expedition window — midnight sun, maximum ice-free coastal access for small vessels, polar bear activity on remaining pack ice, and walrus haul-outs on the remote northern coasts of Svalbard. The Lofoten Islands and Norwegian coast are at their most accessible and most dramatic in summer. Greenland's west coast expedition season peaks in July and August. Iceland in summer delivers the full landscape experience with twenty-two hours of usable daylight — the midnight sun photography window is extraordinary.
Northern Lights Season
September – March
The aurora borealis requires darkness, which means the primary northern lights window runs from late September — when the Arctic night returns after the midnight sun period — through late March. Peak activity occurs when the KP index exceeds 3 and the sky is clear. Tromsø, northern Iceland, and the Norwegian Arctic interior provide the best combination of dark skies, accessible logistics, and reliable infrastructure for northern lights pursuit. Iceland's ice caves in Vatnajökull glacier are accessible November through March, adding a winter activity that justifies the cold entirely.

Common Questions

Polar Regions — FAQ

Questions about Antarctic expeditions, Arctic travel, expedition ship selection, and what to actually expect on a polar voyage.

How physically demanding is an Antarctic expedition?+
The main expedition activity — Zodiac landing operations — requires stepping in and out of small inflatable boats onto rocky beaches or ice, sometimes in rough water. Walking on uneven terrain is involved. Most healthy adults in reasonable shape manage it. Kayaking, camping on the ice, and snowshoeing are optional add-ons for those who want more physical engagement. The ships themselves are extremely comfortable.
What is the difference between Arctic and Antarctic travel?+
Antarctica is pure wilderness — no permanent human population, millions of penguins, extraordinary icebergs, and a sense of genuine remoteness. The Arctic offers polar bears in the High Arctic and access to inhabited destinations like Svalbard, Iceland, and Greenland's west coast. Both are extraordinary; Antarctica tends to have the stronger emotional impact for first-time polar travelers.
How do I choose an expedition cruise ship?+
Key variables: ship size (under 150 passengers allows landings at more sites and provides a more intimate experience), expedition team quality (guides, ornithologists, geologists, lecturers), Zodiac-to-passenger ratio, and onboard facilities. Top operators — Ponant, Silversea Expeditions, Hurtigruten, Aurora Expeditions — are meaningfully different from large mass-market alternatives.
When is the best time to visit Antarctica?+
The Antarctic season runs November through March. November offers pristine snow landscapes and penguin courtship. December–January provides the greatest wildlife activity — penguin chicks and seal pups. February–March brings better whale activity and is slightly calmer for Drake Passage crossings. Weather is a reality of polar travel at any point in the season.
What is "fly-cruise" and is it worth the extra cost?+
A fly-cruise uses a charter flight from Punta Arenas, Chile to King George Island, bypassing the Drake Passage and saving 4 days of transit. It costs significantly more and eliminates the Drake crossing — which many travelers describe as an iconic part of the experience in itself. I advise on the trade-off based on total trip length and individual preference.
What should I pack for an Antarctic expedition?+
Most operators provide heavy-weight parkas and waterproof boots on-property. What you bring: merino wool or synthetic base layers, mid layers, waterproof pants for Zodiac operations, a balaclava, liner gloves, heavy gloves, and seasickness medication. Camera gear protection is important — lenses and sensors are vulnerable to salt spray and rapid temperature changes between the ship and the deck.
How far in advance should I book an Antarctic voyage?+
Popular departures on the smaller, more sought-after ships book 12–18 months in advance. The window is not as long as it might seem — Antarctica has only one season per year, and the best ships have limited capacity. Early booking also typically delivers the best cabin selection. I can sometimes find last-minute availability at significant discounts, but this is not a reliable strategy for most travelers.
Can I combine Antarctica with other South American destinations?+
Yes, and most clients do. Departures are typically from Ushuaia, Argentina — the world's southernmost city — and the broader Patagonia region (Torres del Paine, the Perito Moreno Glacier) is an extraordinary add-on. Buenos Aires and the Argentine wine country (Mendoza) also combine well. A full Southern South America and Antarctica itinerary typically runs 18–24 days.
