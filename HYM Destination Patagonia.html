South America

The End of the Earth,
Worth Every Mile.

Patagonia is the landscape that travelers consistently describe as having no reference point — the granite spires of Torres del Paine, the Perito Moreno glacier advancing six feet a day into Lago Argentino, the constant wind that makes the steppe feel actively alive. The planning work here is choosing the lodge-supported trek over the tent, the right week against weather variability, and the Chilean and Argentine sides as a combined circuit rather than separate trips.

The Region

Weather Is the Point,
Not the Problem.

Patagonia's weather is famously unpredictable — four seasons in a day is the cliché and it is accurate. The wind that scrapes the Torres del Paine valleys can reach 100 km/h in mid-summer; the same valley two hours later can be glass-still under sunshine. The mistake travelers make is to treat the variability as something to overcome. It isn't. It's what makes Patagonia feel genuinely wild rather than packaged. The planning work is positioning yourself in lodges close enough to the trailheads that you can move when the window opens and rest when it doesn't.

The lodge-supported trek (sleeping in mountain refuges along the W-circuit, hot showers, wine with dinner, no tent) is not a compromise version of Patagonia. The refuges inside Torres del Paine National Park are well-built, well-staffed, and the difference between sleeping in one and sleeping in a tent is significant in comfort but not in wilderness exposure — the trail by day is identical. For approximately 80% of travelers, the lodge-supported W-trek is the correct format. The tent version adds physical hardship without adding meaningful wilderness value for travelers who aren't already serious backcountry hikers.

The Chilean and Argentine sides of Patagonia are different countries with different geographies. Chile holds the Torres del Paine massif and the southern fjord-and-channel coastline. Argentina holds the Perito Moreno glacier, the Fitz Roy and Cerro Torre spires above El Chaltén, and Tierra del Fuego with the southernmost city in the world at Ushuaia. The combined circuit — Chilean Torres del Paine plus Argentine Perito Moreno plus Fitz Roy — is the comprehensive Patagonia experience. Doing only one side is a common compromise that loses half the landscape.

Six Patagonian Landscapes Worth Planning Around

Patagonia is divided unevenly between Chile and Argentina, with the genuinely extraordinary sites concentrated across a 500-mile corridor in the south. Each has a specific season, a specific lodge, and a version of the experience worth flying south for.

Destination Photography · Torres del Paine
Chilean Patagonia
Torres del Paine
The W-Trek and the full circuit (the O-Trek, 8–9 days around the Paine massif): lodge-supported options that eliminate the tent exist and produce a categorically different experience from camping. The Torres themselves at dawn from the refuge at the base of the ascent — the granite towers turn from black to amber in twenty minutes as the sun crests the eastern ridges and lights the west face. Los Cuernos (the Horns), the French Valley with its hanging glacier above the valley floor, Grey Glacier viewable by kayak across the lake from a lodge on its eastern shore. The signature Patagonia destination and the one that anchors most first-visit itineraries.

Destination Photography · Perito Moreno
Argentine Patagonia
Perito Moreno Glacier
One of the few glaciers in Patagonia that isn't retreating — it advances approximately six feet per day into Lago Argentino, where it calves dramatically off a face that stretches three miles wide and stands 250 feet above the water. Ice trekking on the glacier surface with crampons (one-hour and full-day options exist; the full-day is significantly more rewarding). The calving face is visible from a series of boardwalks at close range — masses of ice the size of buildings break off and crash into the lake with a delay between the visual and the sound that travelers consistently underestimate. Based out of El Calafate, an hour's drive from the glacier entrance.

Destination Photography · El Chaltén
Argentine Patagonia
El Chaltén & Fitz Roy
Argentina's trekking capital and the gateway to the Fitz Roy massif. Laguna de los Tres trail for the iconic Fitz Roy reflection at dawn — an 8-hour round trip that ascends 1,100 meters and ends at a glacial lake mirroring the granite spires. Laguna Torre for the Cerro Torre spire (a more technical climbing objective, but the trail to the viewpoint is more moderate). A village whose entire infrastructure — restaurants, gear shops, lodges, transport — is oriented toward trekkers rather than package tourists. Three to four nights here pairs naturally with three nights in El Calafate for the Perito Moreno glacier visit, connected by a 3-hour drive on Ruta 40.

Destination Photography · Ushuaia
Tierra del Fuego
Tierra del Fuego & Ushuaia
The southernmost city in the world (54.8° S), and the embarkation point for Antarctic expedition cruises. The Beagle Channel by zodiac with a Magellanic penguin colony visible from the water (October to March). The Martial Glacier above the city, reached by chairlift to the base and a half-day hike to the snout. The Tren del Fin del Mundo — the End of the World train — through the forested approach to Tierra del Fuego National Park. Ushuaia adds two nights to a Patagonia itinerary and shifts the geographic feeling of the trip; for travelers planning to combine with an Antarctic cruise, this is the boarding port.

Atlantic Patagonia
Peninsula Valdés
Right whale watching from September through November — southern right whales calve in the sheltered Gulf of San José and the Gulf Nuevo, and the boats from Puerto Pirámides regularly approach within a few meters of mothers and calves. Orca hunting sea lions at Punta Norte from October through November — a behavior unique to this stretch of coast, where the orcas have learned to beach themselves intentionally to grab pinniped pups, then ride the receding wave back into deep water. Magellanic penguin colony at Punta Tombo (the largest in continental South America). A standalone Atlantic Patagonia destination, accessible by flight from Buenos Aires to Trelew.

Destination Photography · Carretera Austral
Chilean Fjords
Chilean Fjords & Carretera Austral
The Navimag ferry route between Puerto Montt and Puerto Natales through the Chilean fjord channels — a 4-day passage that is among the world's great slow-travel experiences, passing glaciers calving into the sea and uninhabited fjord coast for hundreds of kilometers. The Carretera Austral by 4WD for the least-visited Chile — Patagonian Andes mountain villages, the Marble Caves of Lago General Carrera (turquoise water-carved chambers in calcium carbonate cliffs), Cerro Castillo National Park as a quieter, equally dramatic alternative to Torres del Paine. For travelers who have done the standard Patagonia circuit and want to go deeper.

What We Add to Patagonia Planning

Patagonia is the destination where weather management, lodge positioning, and Chilean–Argentine logistics combine to produce a trip that either flows or constantly fights itself. Getting the structure right is most of the work.

⬡
Weather Window Management
Patagonia's weather is famously unpredictable. A four-day Torres del Paine trek can include sun, rain, snow, and 100 km/h wind in the same window. The right lodge selection matters enormously because the trekker who can wake up at a refuge inside the park can move when the weather window opens, while the day-tripper from Puerto Natales can lose a day to road delays. We build itineraries with weather buffer baked in and choose lodges that maximize trail flexibility.
⬡
Chilean vs Argentine Entry Logic
Most travelers fly into Santiago and out of Buenos Aires (or reverse), which determines the most efficient direction through the Patagonia circuit. Chile's Torres del Paine pairs with Argentina's El Calafate and El Chaltén via a single border crossing — but the order matters for the lodge availability calendar and for how the trip's emotional arc progresses. We sequence the order to match each client's specific arc and the lodge calendar.
⬡
Lodge Trek vs Camping
The lodge-supported W-Trek is not a compromise version of Patagonia trekking. The quality of the refuges inside Torres del Paine has improved dramatically over the past decade — hot showers, three-course dinners, wine with the meal, well-built mountain dormitory rooms with proper bedding. The trail experience by day is identical to the camping version. For approximately 80% of travelers, the lodge version is the correct format. We're honest with the remaining 20% about when tent-camping adds something real.
⬡
Torres at Dawn Logistics
The classic photograph of the Torres del Paine spires at first light requires a hike that begins at 2:30am from Refugio Chileno, ascends a steep boulder field in the dark, and arrives at the lake at the base of the Towers approximately 30 minutes before dawn. The lodge selection at the base of this trail matters more than at any other point in the circuit — staying at Chileno cuts the pre-dawn approach from 3 hours to 90 minutes and dramatically increases the probability of being at the lake when the light hits.
When to Go

Patagonia by Season

Southern Summer
November – March
The only realistic window for the full Patagonia trekking circuit. Daylight extends 18+ hours at the southern latitudes, all trails are open, all lodges are operating, and the temperature in the valleys runs 10–18°C — cool but workable. Wind remains the dominant weather variable throughout, but rain frequency drops compared to the shoulder months. December and January are peak season for international tourism; book the W-Trek lodges 6+ months in advance for these months. November is excellent and noticeably less crowded. March offers the autumn-color version of the landscape (lenga and ñire trees turn red and gold) and trails that are still open.
Best Overall Window
Shoulder & Peak Edge
November / Late March
November is the shoulder window before the December–January peak — wildflowers across the steppe, snow still on the higher trails of Torres del Paine in early November (which limits the full O-Trek but keeps the standard W-Trek accessible), and lodge availability significantly better than peak. Late March into early April produces the autumn colors of the southern beech (lenga) forests — a particularly photogenic version of the landscape. The first half of April is the practical end of the trekking season as lodges begin closing for winter.
Off Season
April – October
Most Patagonia lodges close from late April through early October. The trekking infrastructure is largely seasonal — refuges close, trails are buried under snow, and the high passes of the O-Trek are dangerous or impassable. The exception: winter Patagonia for serious adventure travelers interested in ski mountaineering, ice climbing, or the dramatic light conditions of the short winter days. Most travelers should not consider Patagonia outside the November–March window. Ushuaia and Buenos Aires remain accessible year-round; the trekking heart of Patagonia is not.

Common Questions

Patagonia — FAQ

Questions we hear most when planning Patagonia — from W-Trek fitness requirements to the Chilean–Argentine debate, the lodge vs. camping format, and weather management.

What is the fitness requirement for the W-Trek?+
The lodge-supported W-Trek is moderate-to-challenging — 5 days of hiking with 12–18 km per day and elevation gain of 500–1,100m on the longer days. Any traveler comfortable with full-day hiking on uneven terrain in variable weather will manage it without difficulty. The dawn ascent to the base of the Towers on the trek's signature day is the hardest single hike — a 4-hour round trip from Chileno with a steep boulder field at the top. We can advise on training in the months leading up to the trip and on modified routes for travelers who want the lodge experience without the most demanding sections.
What is the best time to visit Patagonia?+
November through March, with November being the under-recognized sweet spot. December and January are peak — best weather odds, longest daylight, and the most reliable lodge operation, but most crowded and requiring earliest bookings. November offers excellent conditions with significantly fewer trekkers and easier lodge availability. February and March remain excellent, with March adding autumn color to the landscape. Outside this window, most of the infrastructure is closed and the trekking circuit is genuinely not accessible.
Lodge-supported W-Trek versus camping — which is right?+
For approximately 80% of travelers, the lodge-supported version is unambiguously the correct format. The mountain refuges inside Torres del Paine National Park have improved dramatically and offer hot showers, comfortable bedding, three-course dinners, and wine with the meal. The trail experience during the day is identical to the tent version. The case for camping is exclusively for travelers who specifically want the backcountry-sleeping experience or who have a strong preference for tent life. The lodges do not feel commercial — they are well-positioned, well-staffed, and embedded in the wilderness in a way that adds rather than subtracts.
Chilean versus Argentine Patagonia — which should I prioritize?+
If forced to choose one for a single-region trip: Chile for Torres del Paine, which is the most iconic Patagonia landscape and the destination most travelers fly south for. But the combined circuit (Torres del Paine + Perito Moreno + Fitz Roy) is dramatically more comprehensive than either side alone, and the additional logistics of the cross-border journey are well-managed. For a first Patagonia trip with 12+ days available, the combined circuit is the right structure. For shorter trips of 7–10 days, focus on one side.
Is Buenos Aires worth adding to a Patagonia trip?+
Yes — two or three nights before or after the wilderness portion adds significant value and provides natural decompression. Buenos Aires has a genuinely world-class restaurant scene (the parrilla tradition at its peak, the modern Argentine cooking at restaurants like Don Julio or Tegui, the Italian-Argentine heritage at the cantinas), an excellent tango scene if approached with the right guide rather than a tourist show, and a city-walk culture that pairs well as a rest before or after a strenuous trekking trip. Most Patagonia clients who add Buenos Aires comment that it was a stronger part of the trip than expected.
How far in advance should I book the W-Trek lodges?+
For November and March travel: 4–6 months ahead is typically sufficient. For December and January (peak): 8–12 months ahead, particularly for Refugio Chileno (which is the smallest of the W-Trek lodges and the most important for the dawn-of-the-Towers hike). The lodges open booking calendars roughly a year in advance and the most popular nights at Chileno specifically can sell out within weeks of the calendar opening. We track this for clients planning more than a year ahead.
Is altitude a concern in Patagonia?+
No — Patagonia is at low elevation throughout the trekking circuit. Torres del Paine valleys are 200–900m, and even the high passes on the O-Trek don't exceed 1,200m. This is dramatically different from the Andes in Peru, where Cusco at 3,400m and Machu Picchu at 2,400m require active altitude acclimatization. Patagonia has no altitude planning component — the demanding factor is weather, wind, and trail length, not thin air.
Can I combine Patagonia with Peru?+
Yes, and it's a frequent combination — South America's two most iconic landscapes paired into a single 3-week trip. The logical structure is Peru first (Machu Picchu, Sacred Valley, Lima), then south to Patagonia. The flight connections through Santiago or Lima are efficient. The seasonality lines up — Peru's dry season (May–October) overlaps modestly with the beginning of Patagonia's summer season (November–March), so the most efficient combined window is October–November for travelers who want both with minimum compromise on either.
