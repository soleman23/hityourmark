The Destination

The Country That
Surrounds the Ruin.

Peru is a country where the headline attraction is extraordinary but everything around it is equally deserving. Machu Picchu is genuinely one of the great archaeological sites on earth — the Inca city hidden in the cloud forest at 7,972 feet, never found by the Spanish, visited by Hiram Bingham in 1911 and mostly intact. The sunrise entry through the Sun Gate, the Temple of the Sun aligned to the winter solstice, the agricultural terraces still following the contours of the mountain — it earns every photograph that has ever been taken of it.

But the Sacred Valley of the Incas — the Urubamba River valley between Cusco and Machu Picchu — is itself one of the richest living cultural landscapes in South America. The Sunday market at Pisac still functions as a trade market for Quechua communities, not primarily as a tourist market. The salt terraces at Maras have been producing salt using pre-Inca irrigation since before the Spanish arrived. The textile cooperative at Chinchero produces work whose natural dyeing process — from plant to finished fabric in a morning — is a direct continuation of techniques that predate the Inca empire.

Altitude acclimatization is a planning element, not a risk. Cusco sits at 11,000 feet — genuinely high, and genuinely manageable with the correct sequence of arrivals. The Sacred Valley, at 9,000 feet, is lower than Cusco and should come first. Two nights in the Valley before Cusco is the correct protocol, and it's built into every itinerary we plan. The altitude question is not a reason to hesitate about Peru. It's a reason to plan properly.

What We Add to Peru Planning

Peru's planning variables — altitude sequence, Machu Picchu ticket timing, Lima food reservations, and guide type — require specific answers before anything else is booked.

⬡
Altitude Management
Cusco at 11,000 feet is genuinely high — acute mountain sickness affects a meaningful percentage of visitors who arrive without acclimatization. The correct sequence is Lima first (sea level), then the Sacred Valley (9,000 feet) for two nights before Cusco (11,000 feet), then Machu Picchu (7,972 feet). Coca tea and hydration through the valley days. When altitude medication (acetazolamide) is appropriate versus when it's unnecessary. We brief every Peru client on this protocol before departure and build the itinerary around it from the start.
⬡
Machu Picchu Entry Tickets
The Peruvian Ministry of Culture has implemented a timed entry system for Machu Picchu with specific circuit options and maximum daily visitor caps. The Sunrise Group ticket — which places you at the Sun Gate as dawn breaks over the site — sells out months in advance for the June-August peak season. Booking the correct entry type for the correct date requires advance planning and a clear understanding of which circuits allow which experiences. We secure these tickets as part of the planning process, not as an afterthought.
⬡
Lima as a Destination
Lima's food scene is genuinely one of the top five cities in the world for cuisine — the product of Gastón Acurio's elevation of Peruvian cooking from local tradition to global standard, and a generation of chefs who trained under him and have now opened their own restaurants. At least two meals in Lima should be planned in advance: one at the Acurio level (Astrid y Gastón, which requires a reservation well in advance) and one on the circuit of neighborhood restaurants that the food guides know and the "best of" lists don't yet. Lima is not a transit city. It's a destination.
⬡
Guide Selection for Inca History
An archaeologist, a licensed Peruvian guide, and a Quechua community guide each provide something different — and the right combination depends on what the traveler wants to understand. The archaeologist explains the astronomical and architectural systems. The licensed guide provides the historical narrative and site logistics. The community guide connects the living Quechua culture to the Inca past in ways that an academic cannot. We build the guide combination for each site based on what the traveler wants to come away with — not on what's cheapest or most logistically convenient.
When to Go

Peru by Season

Dry Season
May – October
Clear views of Machu Picchu and the Sacred Valley from the train and the site itself. The best hiking conditions on the Inca Trail and the upper circuits at the site. Comfortable temperatures across the highlands — cool at night in Cusco (7-10°C), warm during the day (18-22°C). The preferred window for all archaeological circuit travel in Peru.
Best Overall Window
Peak Demand
June – August
The busiest period — Machu Picchu sunrise tickets sell out four to six months in advance, the best Sacred Valley hotels fill equally early, and the Inca Trail lottery for the hiking route requires booking the previous November. The conditions are excellent. The logistics require the most advance planning of any Peru window. Book everything at the same time the trip dates are confirmed — not after flights are purchased.
Wet Season
November – April
Machu Picchu in the wet season has a specific quality — low cloud and mist through the ruins, vivid green vegetation on the terraces, fewer visitors in the valley. The Inca Trail closes entirely in February for maintenance. The Sacred Valley receives most of its annual rainfall between December and March. Lima (at sea level, rarely affected by the highland wet season) is excellent year-round. For Machu Picchu specifically, the wet season is beautiful but requires accepting limited views on some days.

Common Questions

Peru — FAQ

Questions we hear most when planning Peru travel — from altitude sickness management to Machu Picchu ticket timing, Lima food access, and how to combine Peru with neighboring countries.

How do I prevent and manage altitude sickness in Peru?+
The primary prevention is the correct arrival sequence: Lima at sea level, then Sacred Valley (9,000 feet) for two nights before Cusco (11,000 feet). Arriving directly to Cusco from Lima is the most common mistake — it compresses the acclimatization into hours rather than days. In the Valley, drink water consistently, avoid alcohol on the first two nights, and take the activity level easy on day one. Coca tea (universally available) provides mild symptomatic relief. Acetazolamide (Diamox) is appropriate for travelers with a history of altitude sensitivity or those on compressed schedules — discuss with a travel health physician before departure. Machu Picchu at 7,972 feet is lower than Cusco and is manageable after proper acclimatization.
How far in advance should I book Machu Picchu tickets?+
For the June-August peak season: four to six months in advance for sunrise group tickets, which sell out fastest. For shoulder season (May, September, October): two to three months is generally sufficient for the entry you want, though earlier is always safer. The Peruvian government's ticketing system releases blocks on a rolling basis; we monitor availability as part of our planning process and secure tickets immediately when travel dates are confirmed. Do not wait until the rest of the trip is booked to start the Machu Picchu ticket process — the ticket should be the first booking made after flight dates are fixed.
Inca Trail vs train to Machu Picchu — which is better?+
The Inca Trail is a four-day hiking route through cloud forest and mountain passes, arriving at Machu Picchu through the Sun Gate at dawn on day four — the most dramatically earned entrance to the site. It's capped at 500 people per day (including guides and porters), and the hiking permit lottery closes in November for the following year's season. It requires solid physical fitness and preparation. The train is a 3.5-hour comfortable journey through the Sacred Valley ending at Aguas Calientes, with a bus to the site. Both arrive at the same Machu Picchu. The trail earns it; the train delivers it. The right choice depends on fitness, time, and what the trip is for.
Is the Lima food scene actually worth planning around?+
Yes, unambiguously. Lima is consistently ranked among the top five cities in the world for cuisine — a ranking driven not by a single famous restaurant but by a broad ecosystem of serious cooking at every price point. Astrid y Gastón (the Acurio flagship) requires a reservation booked before departure. La Mar for ceviche at noon (no reservations, arrive at opening). Isolina for the anticucho and the comfort Peruvian food that visitors to Lima rarely find. The Miraflores and Barranco restaurant neighborhoods each have half a dozen places worth eating. Lima deserves at minimum two full meals planned in advance. Most Peru trips use it only as a transit night and miss the best food in South America.
Is Lake Titicaca worth adding to a Peru trip?+
For a traveler who has time (two nights minimum beyond the Cusco/Machu Picchu circuit), yes. The Lake Titicaca extension adds a pre-Inca cultural layer — the Uro people predate the Inca empire and have a distinct origin story and material culture — and a landscape that has no equivalent elsewhere on the trip. The altitude (12,500 feet, higher than Cusco) requires that the Titicaca extension come after the Cusco acclimatization is complete, not before. For a traveler on a ten-day trip, Titicaca is a stretch. For a fourteen-to-sixteen day trip, it's the right addition.
What is the Amazon logistics for a Peru trip?+
The most accessible Peru Amazon option is Puerto Maldonado — a 45-minute flight from Cusco, then a 45-minute boat transfer to a jungle lodge in the Tambopata National Reserve. A three-to-four night stay covers the oxbow lake (giant otters), the macaw clay lick at dawn, night walks for caiman and tarantulas, and the canopy tower for birds at sunrise. Manu National Park is significantly more remote and genuinely more biodiverse, but requires a multi-day overland transfer that adds complexity to a standard Peru itinerary. We recommend the Puerto Maldonado option for most travelers and the Manu option for travelers who specifically want the most pristine Amazon access available.
What should I budget for a Peru trip?+
Peru is generally more affordable than comparable-depth South American trips in Brazil or Argentina at the luxury end. A meaningful twelve-day Peru trip (Lima boutique hotel, Sacred Valley lodge, Cusco hotel, Machu Picchu Belmond or equivalent, Inca Trail guide or private train, specialist guides throughout) runs approximately $6,000-10,000 per person excluding international flights. The Lima food circuit adds relatively little to the total budget — Lima's best restaurants are excellent value by international standards. Adding Lake Titicaca or the Amazon adds cost primarily through the additional nights and transportation.
Is solo female travel comfortable in Peru?+
Yes, with standard urban awareness that applies to any South American city. Lima and Cusco are both manageable as solo destinations with the usual precautions — taking registered taxis, avoiding carrying valuables openly in crowded markets, being aware of which neighborhoods to avoid at night. The Sacred Valley and the Machu Picchu corridor are established tourism environments with consistent safety infrastructure. The Amazon lodges are controlled environments where solo travelers are well-provided for. Peru has a well-developed solo female traveler infrastructure in the main tourism corridors. We brief all solo clients specifically on current conditions and specific neighborhoods before departure.
